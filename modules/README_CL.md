# Credit and Loans: What They Are and How They Work

**Module:** Credit and Loans  
**Framework:** Financial Literacy Framework

## Why This Module Exists

Millions of Americans avoid credit entirely because they do not understand it — or they take on credit without understanding the cost. The result is the same in both cases: people pay more, build less, and have fewer choices over time.

This module does not assume you already know what APR means or why your credit score matters. It starts from the beginning, uses plain language, and explains the real-world consequences of every concept it introduces.

## What You Will Learn

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Explain what credit is and why lenders offer it
- Read a loan offer and identify the actual cost of borrowing
- Understand what goes into a credit score and what changes it
- Recognize the difference between credit that helps you build and credit that traps you
- Know your rights when you are denied credit or when something goes wrong

## Section 1: What Is Credit?

Credit is an agreement to use money now and pay it back later, usually with a fee called interest.

When a bank or lender gives you a credit card or a loan, they are trusting you to repay it. That trust is based on your credit history — a record of how you have handled borrowed money in the past. If you have no credit history, lenders have no record to look at, which makes it harder (not impossible) to get started.

**Three things every credit product has:**

1. **Principal** — the amount you borrow
2. **Interest rate** — the percentage you pay to use the money
3. **Repayment terms** — how long you have to pay it back and in what amounts

**Example:**  
You borrow $1,000 at 12% interest per year, repaid over 12 months.  
Your monthly payment is approximately $88.85.  
At the end of 12 months, you will have paid back the $1,000 plus about $66 in interest.  
The total cost of borrowing is $1,066.

That $66 is the price you paid to use someone else's money for a year. Whether that is a good deal depends on why you needed the money and whether you had other options.

## Section 2: Types of Credit

Not all credit works the same way. Understanding the type matters before you sign anything.

### Revolving Credit
You have a spending limit. You can borrow up to that limit, repay it, and borrow again.

- **Credit cards** are the most common example
- The minimum payment is usually small, but only paying the minimum means you pay interest on the rest
- If you carry a balance month to month, the cost adds up quickly

**Example:**  
$1,000 balance on a card at 22% APR.  
If you pay only the $25 minimum each month, it takes over 5 years to pay off and costs more than $500 in interest.  
If you pay $100 per month, it takes about 11 months and costs around $100 in interest.

### Installment Credit
You borrow a fixed amount and repay it in equal payments over a set period.

- Auto loans, personal loans, student loans, and mortgages are all installment loans
- The payment amount and end date are fixed from the start
- Easier to budget because nothing changes month to month

### Secured vs. Unsecured Credit
- **Secured credit** is backed by something you own (collateral). If you stop paying, the lender can take it. Mortgages and auto loans are secured.
- **Unsecured credit** is backed only by your promise to repay. Credit cards and most personal loans are unsecured. Because the lender takes more risk, the interest rate is usually higher.

## Section 3: Understanding Interest Rates

The interest rate is the cost of borrowing money. It is usually shown as an annual percentage rate, or APR.

**APR includes:**
- The base interest rate
- Most fees the lender charges

This is why comparing APRs is more useful than comparing interest rates alone.

### How Interest Accumulates

For most credit cards, interest is calculated daily based on your average balance. The daily rate is your APR divided by 365.

**Example:**  
$500 balance at 18% APR.  
Daily rate: 18% ÷ 365 = 0.049% per day.  
After 30 days with no payment: approximately $7.40 in interest added.

That number looks small. But if you never pay the balance down, you are paying $7.40 every month — $88 per year — just to hold that $500 balance.

### Fixed vs. Variable Rates

- **Fixed rate:** stays the same for the life of the loan or a defined period
- **Variable rate:** changes based on a benchmark rate (usually tied to the federal funds rate)

Variable rates can rise. If your credit card has a variable rate and interest rates go up nationally, your rate goes up too — even on money you already borrowed.

## Section 4: Your Credit Score

A credit score is a three-digit number that summarizes how reliably you have repaid borrowed money. Lenders use it to decide whether to lend to you and at what rate.

### The FICO Score (Most Common Range: 300–850)

| Score Range | Category |
|---|---|
| 800–850 | Exceptional |
| 740–799 | Very Good |
| 670–739 | Good |
| 580–669 | Fair |
| Below 580 | Poor |

*Source: myFICO.com*

A higher score means lenders see you as lower risk. Lower risk means lower interest rates — which means you pay less to borrow the same amount.

**Example of why this matters:**  
$30,000 auto loan over 60 months:
- At 4.5% APR (excellent credit): monthly payment ≈ $559, total interest ≈ $3,540
- At 14.5% APR (fair credit): monthly payment ≈ $703, total interest ≈ $12,180

Same loan. Same car. The difference in interest: over $8,600.

### What Goes Into Your Credit Score

Credit scoring models vary, but FICO — the most widely used — weights five factors:

1. **Payment history (35%)** — Do you pay on time? This is the single biggest factor. One 30-day late payment can drop your score significantly.
2. **Amounts owed (30%)** — How much of your available credit are you using? Using more than 30% of your credit limit can hurt your score even if you pay on time.
3. **Length of credit history (15%)** — How long have your accounts been open? Older accounts help.
4. **Credit mix (10%)** — Do you have different types of credit (card, loan, mortgage)? A mix can help, but do not open accounts you do not need just for this.
5. **New credit (10%)** — Have you recently applied for new credit? Multiple applications in a short period can signal financial stress and lower your score temporarily.

### How to Build Credit from Zero

If you have no credit history, here are the lowest-risk starting points:

- **Secured credit card** — You deposit money as collateral. The deposit becomes your credit limit. Use it for small purchases, pay the full balance every month. After 6–12 months, most issuers graduate you to an unsecured card and return your deposit.
- **Credit-builder loan** — Offered by many credit unions. You make payments on a loan, but the money goes into a savings account you receive at the end. The on-time payments are reported to credit bureaus and build your history.
- **Becoming an authorized user** — A family member or trusted person adds you to their existing credit card. Their payment history on that account appears on your credit report. You do not need to use the card.

## Section 5: Common Credit Traps

Knowing the product is not enough. You also need to know what to watch for.

### Payday Loans
Short-term loans (usually 2 weeks) at extremely high fees. A $15 fee on a $100 loan sounds small until you calculate the APR: approximately 390%. If you cannot repay and roll it over, fees can exceed the original loan quickly.

**Safer alternative:** Many credit unions offer small-dollar emergency loans at rates under 28% APR, sometimes called payday alternative loans (PALs).

### Buy Now, Pay Later (BNPL)
Splitting purchases into installments, often at 0% interest — but:
- Late payments often trigger fees or full interest retroactively
- BNPL providers increasingly report to credit bureaus, so a missed payment affects your score
- Having multiple BNPL plans running at once is easy to lose track of

### Credit Repair Companies
Companies that promise to fix your credit for a fee are largely unregulated in their claims. No company can legally remove accurate negative information from your credit report before its natural expiration date. What a credit repair company can do, you can do yourself for free.

## Section 6: Your Rights

Federal law gives you specific rights when it comes to credit.

### The Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA)
- You can get a free copy of your credit report from each of the three major bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) once every 12 months at **AnnualCreditReport.com**
- If something on your report is wrong, you have the right to dispute it — and the bureau must investigate and remove inaccurate information
- Negative accurate information (late payments, collections) generally falls off your report after 7 years. Bankruptcies may remain for up to 10 years.

### The Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA)
A lender cannot deny you credit based on race, color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age, or because you receive public assistance.

If you are denied credit, the lender must tell you why — specifically. "Your credit score was too low" is not a sufficient explanation under the law. They must tell you which factor was determinative.

### The Truth in Lending Act (TILA)
Before you sign, lenders must disclose:
- The APR
- The total amount you will pay over the life of the loan
- The monthly payment
- Any fees

If a lender will not give you these numbers in writing before you sign, walk away.

## Section 7: Practical Checklist Before Taking On Credit

Before you sign a credit agreement, ask yourself:

- [ ] Do I know the exact APR, not just the interest rate?
- [ ] Do I know the total cost of this loan (principal + all interest + fees)?
- [ ] Do I know the monthly payment and can I afford it if my income drops?
- [ ] Have I compared at least two other lenders?
- [ ] Do I know what happens if I miss a payment?
- [ ] Is this a need or a want — and is this the best way to pay for it?
