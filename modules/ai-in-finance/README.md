# Module: AI in Finance

**For educators, financial counsellors, and member-facing staff**

This module helps financial institution members understand what artificial intelligence means for their financial lives — how it is being used, what decisions it is influencing, and what rights they have when AI affects them.

This is the module no other financial literacy curriculum has yet built. AI is now making or informing decisions about loan approvals, fraud flags, investment recommendations, and credit scores. Members who do not understand this cannot advocate for themselves.

---

## Who This Module Is For

This module is most valuable for:
- Members who have been denied credit or flagged for fraud and want to understand why
- First-generation banking customers navigating financial products for the first time
- Anyone who wants to understand how technology is changing financial decision-making
- Members considering products that use AI-powered features

## Core Concepts

### Concept 1: AI Is Already Making Decisions About Your Money

When you apply for a loan, the lender often uses a computer program to help decide whether to approve you. When your debit card is declined because of suspected fraud, an algorithm made that call. When an investment app recommends a portfolio for you, AI built the recommendation.

This is not the future. It is happening now, at the institution where you have your account.

**What this means for you:** Decisions about your money are increasingly made by systems that process data about you very quickly — your income, your spending history, your location, your credit file. These systems can be very accurate. They can also make mistakes. And unlike a human loan officer, an algorithm will not pick up the phone to hear your explanation.

**Plain language key point:** An AI system decided something about your money using rules and your data. You did not get to talk to it. But you do have rights.

---

### Concept 2: You Have the Right to Know Why a Decision Was Made

Under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) and the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), financial institutions must tell you the specific reasons for any adverse decision about credit — including when an AI or algorithm was involved.

A vague answer is not acceptable. "Your application did not meet our criteria" is not a specific reason. Specific reasons look like this: "Your debt-to-income ratio is above our threshold" or "Your credit score is below the minimum required for this product."

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has explicitly said that using AI to make lending decisions does not excuse institutions from providing specific, accurate adverse action reasons.

**Questions you can ask your institution:**
- Was an automated system involved in this decision?
- Can you tell me specifically which factors led to this outcome?
- Can I speak with someone to discuss my application?
- How can I improve my application for the future?

---

### Concept 3: AI Systems Can Be Wrong — Including About You

AI systems learn from historical data. Historical financial data reflects decades of unequal access to credit, banking, and investment products. When an AI learns from that data, it can reproduce those patterns — not because anyone programmed it to discriminate, but because the history it learned from was unequal.

This is called algorithmic bias. It means that people from certain geographic areas, with certain names, or with certain financial histories may be systematically disadvantaged by AI systems — even when they are perfectly qualified for the product they are applying for.

**What you can do:**
- You can dispute an AI-assisted decision if you believe it was wrong or unfair
- You can file a complaint with the CFPB at consumerfinance.gov/complaint
- You can ask the institution to review your application manually

---

### Concept 4: What "Black Box" Means and Why It Matters

Some AI systems are described as "black boxes" — meaning the institution using them cannot fully explain why the system made a specific decision. They can see that the system recommended a denial. They may not be able to tell you exactly which data points caused it.

This is a known problem. Regulators including the CFPB, OCC, and FINRA have flagged it as a top compliance concern. The law requires explainability. A black-box system that cannot produce a specific explanation is a system that may not be operating legally.

**Key insight for members:** If an institution cannot explain specifically why their AI system made a decision about you, that is worth noting — and potentially worth reporting.

---

### Concept 5: AI-Powered Products That Benefit You

AI in finance is not only a risk. Used responsibly, it can genuinely help members who were previously underserved.

**Examples of beneficial AI in finance:**
- **Alternative credit scoring:** AI can analyse payment patterns beyond traditional credit bureaus — utility payments, rent history, subscription payments — to build a credit picture for thin-file applicants who would otherwise be invisible to traditional scoring
- **Fraud detection:** AI can spot suspicious transactions in real time and block them before money leaves your account
- **Personalised financial guidance:** AI can analyse your spending patterns and suggest ways to save, alert you to unusual charges, or help you plan toward a financial goal
- **Plain-language explanations:** AI tools can explain financial products in simple terms tailored to your situation

**The difference between beneficial and harmful AI in finance:** Whether the AI's decision can be explained and challenged. A fraud alert that protects your account is beneficial. A loan denial that cannot be explained is a problem.

## Glossary — AI in Finance Terms

**Algorithm**
A set of rules a computer follows to make a decision or perform a task. A loan approval algorithm might check your income, credit score, and debt level and compare them to minimum thresholds before producing a recommendation.

**Artificial Intelligence (AI)**
Computer systems that can perform tasks that typically require human intelligence — like recognising patterns, making decisions, or generating recommendations. In finance, AI is used for credit decisions, fraud detection, investment recommendations, and customer service.

**Algorithmic Bias**
When an AI system produces outcomes that are systematically unfair to certain groups of people, often because it was trained on historical data that reflected past discrimination or unequal access.

**Adverse Action Notice**
A written notice you receive when a lender takes a negative action on your credit application or account. It must include the specific reasons for the decision. Federal law requires this for all credit decisions, including AI-assisted ones.

**Black Box**
An AI system whose internal reasoning process cannot be easily explained. The institution using it can see the inputs and outputs but not clearly how it arrived at a decision.

**Explainability**
The ability to describe, in plain language, why an AI system made a specific decision. Regulators increasingly require that AI systems used in financial decisions be explainable to consumers and to examiners.

**Model Drift**
When an AI system's accuracy or fairness declines over time because the real world has changed and the model has not been updated. A loan model trained before a recession may behave differently during one.

## For Educators — How to Teach This Module

**Estimated time:** 60 to 90 minutes for a workshop session

**Key points to emphasise:**
1. AI in finance is not hypothetical — it is affecting decisions right now
2. Members have legal rights to explanations, regardless of how the decision was made
3. AI systems can be wrong and can be biased — and members can push back
4. There are also genuinely beneficial AI applications members can seek out

**Discussion questions for workshop participants:**
- Have you ever received a financial decision you did not understand or agree with?
- Did you try to find out why? What happened?
- What would you want to know about how an AI decides whether to approve your loan?

**Handout to provide:** The glossary from this module, plus the CFPB complaint page URL: consumerfinance.gov/complaint
