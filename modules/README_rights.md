# Understanding Your Rights: What Financial Institutions Owe You

**Module:** Understanding Your Rights  
**Framework:** Financial Literacy Framework  
**Audience:** Anyone who interacts with banks, credit unions, lenders, or fintech platforms; particularly valuable for underserved communities who have historically experienced discriminatory practices  
**Time to complete:** 30–40 minutes  
**Prerequisite:** None required.

---

## Why This Module Exists

Financial institutions are regulated. That regulation exists because, historically, financial institutions have not always treated consumers fairly — particularly consumers from low-income backgrounds, communities of color, or groups that lacked the resources to push back.

Knowing your rights changes your relationship with every financial institution you interact with. You are not asking for a favor when you request a clear explanation of a denial. You are exercising a legal entitlement. You are not accepting poor service when your credit report contains errors. You have the right to dispute them and have them corrected.

This module explains what those rights are, in plain language, and what to do when they are not honored.

## Section 1: Your Core Rights as a Financial Consumer

Several federal laws protect you in your dealings with financial institutions. Here are the ones that affect everyday financial decisions.

---

### The Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA)

**What it does:** Prohibits discrimination in any aspect of a credit transaction based on:
- Race or color
- Religion
- National origin
- Sex
- Marital status
- Age (as long as you are old enough to enter a contract)
- Receipt of public assistance

**What it means for you:** A lender cannot deny you a loan, offer you worse terms, or require extra documentation because of any of these characteristics.

**What you are entitled to:**
- A specific reason for any credit denial within 30 days
- The right to request a statement of reasons if you are not automatically given one

The reason must be specific and accurate. "We don't think you'll pay it back" is not a legally sufficient explanation. "Your debt-to-income ratio exceeds our guidelines" is.

---

### The Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA)

**What it does:** Governs how consumer reporting agencies (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) collect, share, and use your credit information.

**Your key rights under the FCRA:**

**Free access to your reports:** You are entitled to one free credit report from each of the three major bureaus every 12 months through **AnnualCreditReport.com** — the only government-authorized source. (Third-party sites that offer "free" credit reports often require payment or enroll you in services you did not ask for.)

**The right to dispute inaccurate information:** If something on your credit report is wrong — wrong account, wrong payment status, accounts that do not belong to you — you have the right to dispute it. The credit bureau must investigate within 30 days and remove or correct inaccurate information.

**How to file a dispute:**
1. Get your credit reports from AnnualCreditReport.com
2. Identify the error — write down the specific item, the account name, the bureau, and what is wrong
3. Submit a dispute online directly to the bureau (Equifax.com, Experian.com, TransUnion.com each have dispute portals) or by certified mail
4. The bureau must notify the creditor who reported the information. The creditor must investigate and respond.
5. Within 30 days, the bureau must provide results. If your dispute is successful, the error is removed.

**Important:** Accurate negative information — a late payment that really happened, a debt you actually owe — cannot be removed before its legal expiration. Most negative items fall off after 7 years. Bankruptcies may remain up to 10 years. No company can legally remove accurate information early, regardless of what they claim.

**Limits on who can see your credit report:** Employers, landlords, and most businesses can only access your credit report with your permission. Lenders can access it when you apply for credit. You can check your own report as often as you like without it affecting your score.

---

### The Truth in Lending Act (TILA)

**What it does:** Requires lenders to clearly disclose the terms and costs of credit before you sign.

**What must be disclosed:**
- Annual Percentage Rate (APR)
- Total amount financed
- Total of all payments over the life of the loan
- Monthly payment amount
- All fees

These disclosures must be provided in writing before you sign anything. If a lender tells you terms verbally and then the written paperwork says something different, the written paperwork is what you signed — but you have the right to cancel certain types of transactions (like a home equity loan on your primary residence) within three business days under TILA's right of rescission.

---

### The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)

**What it does:** Regulates how third-party debt collectors (companies hired to collect a debt you owe to someone else) can contact you.

**Debt collectors cannot:**
- Call before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. in your time zone
- Call your workplace if you tell them your employer does not allow such calls
- Use abusive, threatening, or obscene language
- Threaten actions they cannot or do not intend to take (like threatening to sue if they have no intention of doing so)
- Misrepresent the amount you owe
- Continue contacting you after you send a written request to stop

**What you can do:**
- Send a written cease-contact letter (by certified mail) and the collector must stop — except to notify you of specific legal actions
- Request in writing that they verify the debt — they must provide documentation
- Dispute the debt within 30 days of first contact

**Note:** The FDCPA applies to third-party collectors, not to the original creditor collecting their own debt. Most states have additional laws that cover original creditors.

---

### The Electronic Fund Transfer Act (EFTA)

**What it does:** Protects consumers in electronic transactions — debit cards, ATMs, direct deposits, and electronic bill payments.

**If your debit card is lost or stolen:**
- Report it within 2 business days: maximum liability is $50
- Report it between 2 and 60 days after your statement: maximum liability is $500
- Report it after 60 days: you may be liable for all unauthorized transactions

This is one of the primary reasons most financial advisors recommend using a credit card (which has stronger fraud protections under TILA) rather than a debit card for everyday purchases. With credit, the money is not yours yet, so disputing a fraudulent charge is simpler.

## Section 2: When AI Makes Decisions About You

AI systems are now being used by financial institutions to make or influence decisions about loan approvals, credit limits, fraud flags, and investment recommendations. This creates new questions about your rights — and regulators are actively addressing them.

### Credit Denials Involving AI

The CFPB has made clear that using AI does not exempt lenders from providing specific, accurate reasons for adverse actions under ECOA.

If an AI model denies your credit application, the lender must still tell you specifically why. "The AI said no" is not an acceptable explanation. The lender must identify which factors — income, debt level, payment history, length of credit history — drove the decision, even if an AI produced it.

*Source: CFPB Circular 2022-03, "Adverse action notification requirements in connection with credit decisions based on complex algorithms."*

### What "Disparate Impact" Means for You

A financial institution does not have to intend to discriminate for discrimination to be illegal. If a practice has a disproportionate negative impact on a protected group — even if it was not designed to — that can constitute illegal discrimination under ECOA and fair lending law.

AI models trained on historical data can inherit historical biases. A model trained on past lending decisions may replicate past discriminatory patterns. Regulators including the CFPB, OCC, and Federal Reserve have each flagged AI bias as a priority enforcement area.

If you believe you were denied credit, charged higher rates, or treated differently in ways that seem connected to your race, national origin, sex, or other protected characteristic — even if the decision was made by an algorithm — you have the right to file a complaint.

### AI in Investment Decisions

The SEC and FINRA have each issued guidance noting that AI-powered investment platforms must still comply with suitability and best-interest standards. An AI that recommends an investment that is not appropriate for your situation, age, or risk tolerance is not excused by the fact that the recommendation came from an algorithm.

If you use a platform that makes investment recommendations or executes trades on your behalf, you are entitled to understand — in plain language — what strategy the AI is using and what risks it involves. If the platform cannot explain this clearly, that is a meaningful warning sign.

## Section 3: How to File a Complaint

When a financial institution violates your rights, you have several places to turn. Filing a complaint matters — it creates a record, and regulators track complaint patterns to identify systemic problems.

### Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)
**consumerfinance.gov/complaint**  
Handles complaints about banks, credit unions, lenders, debt collectors, credit bureaus, and many fintech platforms. The CFPB forwards your complaint to the company and requires them to respond within 15 days. Most complaints receive a substantive response within 60 days.  
You can also call: 1-855-411-2372 (Monday–Friday, 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. ET)

### Federal Trade Commission (FTC)
**reportfraud.ftc.gov**  
Handles complaints about identity theft, unfair or deceptive business practices, and violations of the FDCPA.

### Credit Bureau Dispute Portals
- Equifax: equifax.com/personal/credit-report-services/credit-dispute
- Experian: experian.com/dispute
- TransUnion: transunion.com/credit-disputes

### Your State Attorney General
Every state has an attorney general's office with a consumer protection division. Many states have laws that go further than federal law. If a federal regulator cannot help, your state AG may be able to.

### The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC)
If your complaint involves a national bank (one with "National" in its name, or "N.A." after it), the OCC's Customer Assistance Group handles complaints.  
**helpwithmybank.gov**

## Section 4: Protecting Yourself Proactively

Rights matter most when they are exercised. Here are concrete steps to protect yourself before something goes wrong.

### Review Your Credit Reports Annually
Get all three reports (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) at AnnualCreditReport.com. Look for:
- Accounts you do not recognize
- Incorrect payment statuses (marked late when you paid on time)
- Wrong personal information (wrong name, address, Social Security number)
- Accounts that should have aged off your report but have not

Dispute anything inaccurate immediately.

### Place a Credit Freeze If You Are Not Actively Seeking Credit
A credit freeze prevents anyone — including you — from opening new credit in your name without unfreezing it first. It is free, can be placed and lifted online at each bureau, and is the most effective tool against identity-theft-based credit fraud.

You can still use your existing credit cards and check your own reports. The freeze only affects new applications.

### Keep Records
When you have a financial dispute, documentation is everything. Save:
- Loan agreements and terms, as signed
- Written communications with lenders or collectors
- Records of payments (bank statements, receipts)
- Denial letters — they are legally required to be in writing

Send important communications by certified mail so you have proof of delivery.

### Know What Your Bank or Credit Union Is Required to Provide
Your deposit institution must give you a written statement of fees before you open an account. If fees change, they must notify you in advance. If an error occurs on your account, report it within 60 days of the statement date — the institution has specific legal obligations to investigate.

## Section 5: Financial Rights and Underserved Communities

Some rights exist specifically because certain communities have historically been denied access to financial services or subjected to predatory practices.

### The Community Reinvestment Act (CRA)
Requires banks and certain other depository institutions to help meet the credit needs of the communities in which they operate, including low- and moderate-income neighborhoods. You can look up a bank's CRA rating at ffiec.gov.

### CDFI Institutions
Community Development Financial Institutions — including many credit unions with CDFI designation — are specifically chartered to serve underserved communities. They often offer products that mainstream banks do not: small-dollar emergency loans, credit-builder products, financial counseling. Find a CDFI credit union at ncua.gov or through Inclusiv, the CDFI credit union network.

### Disparate Impact and Fair Lending Enforcement
If you believe a lender is systematically denying credit to people in your neighborhood or community based on race, national origin, or other protected characteristics, you can file a complaint with:
- The CFPB (consumerfinance.gov/complaint)
- The U.S. Department of Justice Civil Rights Division
- Your state's civil rights agency
