# Investing Basics: Making Your Money Work Over Time

**Module:** Investing Basics  
**Framework:** Financial Literacy Framework  
**Audience:** Adults who are saving but not yet investing, credit union members, employees new to workplace retirement plans  
**Time to complete:** 35–45 minutes  
**Prerequisite:** It helps (but is not required) to have completed the Banking Basics and Credit and Loans modules first.

---

## Why This Module Exists

Most Americans are familiar with the idea of a savings account. Fewer understand investing — and many who do understand it feel it is only for wealthy people or financial experts.

This is a design failure, not a knowledge failure. The financial industry has historically communicated investing in a way that made it feel exclusive: complicated terminology, high minimum balances, and products that paid the seller more than they helped the buyer.

This module strips away that complexity. It explains what investing actually is, how it works over time, what the real risks are, and how someone with a small amount of money can start — not by speculating, but by participating in long-term wealth building the way most financial professionals recommend.

## Section 1: Saving vs. Investing

**Saving** means setting money aside where it is safe and accessible. A savings account at a bank or credit union holds your money, earns a small amount of interest, and is protected up to $250,000 by the FDIC or NCUA. The money is there when you need it.

**Investing** means putting money into something — a business, a debt instrument, real estate — with the expectation that it will grow in value over time. That growth is not guaranteed. The value can go down as well as up. In exchange for taking that risk, investors historically have earned returns that significantly exceed what savings accounts pay.

**When to save vs. when to invest:**

Savings is for money you need within the next one to three years — rent, emergency fund, a planned purchase. If the stock market dropped 30% tomorrow and you needed that money next month, you would lose 30% of what you needed.

Investing is for money you can leave untouched for five years or more. Over long periods, markets have historically recovered from downturns and continued growing. The longer the time horizon, the more risk you can absorb.

**The baseline: Build an emergency fund first.** Before you invest, most financial guidance recommends having three to six months of essential expenses in a savings or money market account. This is not because savings is better than investing. It is because without a cushion, an unexpected expense forces you to sell investments at the wrong time.

## Section 2: How Compound Growth Works

Compound growth is the single most important concept in personal investing. Understanding it changes how you think about time.

When you invest and earn a return, the return itself earns future returns. Over time, this effect becomes dramatic.

**Example:**

You invest $2,000 and earn an average of 7% per year. You add no additional money.

| Year | Value |
|---|---|
| 0 | $2,000 |
| 5 | $2,805 |
| 10 | $3,934 |
| 20 | $7,739 |
| 30 | $15,224 |
| 40 | $29,948 |

In 40 years, with no additional contributions, your $2,000 grew to nearly $30,000 — without you doing anything. The money did the work.

**What makes the difference is time, not the amount.**

Now compare two people:
- Person A invests $100 per month starting at age 25, stops at age 35 (10 years of contributions), and leaves it until age 65
- Person B invests $100 per month starting at age 35, all the way until age 65 (30 years of contributions)

Assuming 7% average annual return:
- Person A contributes $12,000 total and ends up with approximately $168,000
- Person B contributes $36,000 total and ends up with approximately $122,000

Person A contributed less money and ended up with more — because they started 10 years earlier.

This is not a trick. It is math. And it is why starting, even with a small amount, matters more than waiting until you have more to invest.

## Section 3: What You Are Investing In

### Stocks (Equities)
When you buy a share of stock, you are buying a small ownership stake in a company. If the company grows and becomes more valuable, your shares are worth more. If it struggles, they are worth less.

Individual stocks are volatile. A single company can rise or fall dramatically based on its own performance, industry changes, or broader economic events. For most investors, owning shares in dozens or hundreds of companies (through funds) is a more stable approach than picking individual stocks.

### Bonds (Fixed Income)
When you buy a bond, you are lending money to a government or corporation. In exchange, they pay you a fixed amount of interest over a set period and return your principal at the end. Bonds are generally less volatile than stocks but also grow more slowly.

Bonds are often held alongside stocks to reduce overall portfolio volatility. As investors get closer to retirement, many shift more of their portfolio toward bonds to protect what they have built.

### Funds (The Most Practical Starting Point for Most Investors)

A fund pools money from many investors and buys a diversified set of stocks, bonds, or both.

**Index funds** are the most widely recommended starting point for most individual investors. They track an index — like the S&P 500, which includes the 500 largest U.S. companies — and simply hold all the stocks in that index in the same proportions. They do not try to beat the market; they match it.

Why index funds? Because research consistently shows that most actively managed funds — funds where professional managers pick individual stocks — do not outperform a simple index fund over long periods, particularly after fees are factored in.

*Source: S&P SPIVA U.S. Scorecard (published annually). As of the 2024 year-end report, more than 85% of actively managed large-cap U.S. equity funds underperformed the S&P 500 over 15-year periods.*

**ETFs (Exchange-Traded Funds)** are index funds that trade on a stock exchange like individual stocks. They can be bought or sold throughout the trading day. Many have very low minimum investment requirements — sometimes as low as the price of one share, or even less through fractional shares.

## Section 4: Understanding Risk

Risk in investing does not mean you will lose everything. It means the value of your investment can go down — sometimes significantly — in the short term, and you cannot know in advance exactly how much or when.

### Types of Risk

**Market risk:** The overall market declines. If the economy enters a recession, nearly all stocks go down, even good companies in strong industries. This is normal and has happened many times historically. Market recoveries have also followed every major decline in U.S. market history, though past performance is not a guarantee of future results.

**Concentration risk:** Too much money in one company, one sector, or one type of investment. If that specific area declines, your whole portfolio suffers. Diversification — owning many different things — reduces concentration risk.

**Inflation risk:** Leaving money in a low-yield savings account means it may be earning less than inflation. If inflation is 3% and your savings account earns 1%, you are losing purchasing power over time. This is a risk of not investing, not of investing.

**Time risk:** The biggest risk for long-term investors is not market volatility. It is selling during a downturn and locking in losses. Investors who stayed invested through the 2008 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic crash and did not sell eventually recovered and continued to grow. Investors who sold near the bottom did not.

### Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance is how much you can afford to lose temporarily without panicking or needing the money. It depends on your time horizon, your financial stability, and your own psychology.

A tool like Vanguard's Investor Questionnaire (free at vanguard.com) asks you a series of questions and suggests an asset allocation — the mix of stocks and bonds — that may fit your situation. This is not a substitute for financial advice, but it is a reasonable starting point.

## Section 5: Retirement Accounts

For most working Americans, the most important investing vehicle is a workplace retirement account. These accounts have tax advantages that significantly improve long-term returns.

### 401(k) (Private Employer)
Offered by private-sector employers. You contribute a portion of your paycheck before taxes are taken out, which reduces your taxable income today. The money grows tax-deferred — you pay taxes when you withdraw in retirement, when you are likely in a lower tax bracket.

**Employer match:** Many employers match a portion of your contribution — for example, 50 cents for every dollar you contribute, up to 6% of your salary. This is free money. Not contributing enough to capture the full match is leaving compensation on the table.

**2024 contribution limit:** $23,000 per year (under age 50). Additional catch-up contributions allowed if 50 or older. *Verify current limits at IRS.gov.*

### Roth 401(k) / Roth IRA
You contribute after-tax dollars. The money grows tax-free, and withdrawals in retirement are tax-free as well. Often better than a traditional 401(k) if you expect to be in a higher tax bracket in retirement than you are now — which is common for younger workers early in their careers.

**Roth IRA income limits:** There are income limits above which you cannot contribute directly to a Roth IRA. Check IRS.gov for current limits; they are adjusted annually.

### What If Your Employer Has No Retirement Plan?
Open an IRA (Individual Retirement Account) directly through a brokerage — Fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard all offer accounts with no minimum balance. A traditional IRA may offer a tax deduction; a Roth IRA offers tax-free growth. Both are available to anyone with earned income.

## Section 6: AI-Powered Investing Tools — What You Should Know

AI is now embedded in many investing platforms. These tools automate portfolio construction, rebalancing, tax optimization, and in some cases, trading itself. Understanding what they do — and what they cannot do — is important before you use one.

### Robo-Advisors
Platforms like Betterment, Wealthfront, and Schwab Intelligent Portfolios use algorithms to build and manage a diversified portfolio based on your risk tolerance and time horizon. You answer a questionnaire, they allocate your money across low-cost index funds, and they rebalance automatically.

These are generally a reasonable option for investors who want a simple, low-cost, hands-off approach. Fees are typically lower than working with a human financial advisor.

What robo-advisors cannot do: give personalized advice about your specific situation, including your tax position, your other assets, or your plans. They manage the portfolio you give them, based on what you tell them.

### Agentic Trading Tools
A newer and more complex category. Platforms are now offering AI agents that can execute trades — and in some cases make purchases — on your behalf with minimal human intervention. These tools can run sophisticated strategies (mean reversion, factor investing, systematic rebalancing) that were previously accessible only through expensive institutional platforms.

The governance questions around these tools are still being worked out across the industry. Key things to understand before using any agent-based investing tool:

- **What can the agent do without your approval?** Read the disclosure carefully. There is a meaningful difference between an agent that shows you a proposed trade and an agent that executes without asking.
- **Who is responsible if it goes wrong?** Platforms typically disclaim responsibility for losses from agent-generated decisions. That responsibility falls on you.
- **What does the agent know about your situation?** Most agents work only with what you tell them in a setup prompt. They do not know your tax position, your other accounts, your income stability, or your full financial picture.
- **Is there a kill switch?** Any responsible agentic investing tool should have a way to disconnect the agent immediately if something goes wrong.

This does not mean agentic tools are bad. It means you should understand exactly what you are authorizing before you authorize it.

## Section 7: How to Start

**Step 1: Open an account.**
If your employer offers a 401(k) with a match, start there. Contribute at least enough to capture the full match. If your employer has no plan, open a Roth IRA at Fidelity, Vanguard, or Schwab. There is no minimum to open the account at most major brokerages.

**Step 2: Choose a starting investment.**
A single target-date fund (named for the year you expect to retire, like "Target Date 2055") or a total market index fund is a reasonable starting point. These are diversified and automatically adjusted over time.

**Step 3: Set up automatic contributions.**
Automatic monthly transfers from your checking account mean you invest consistently without having to decide each time. Even $25 or $50 per month builds real habits and real money over time.

**Step 4: Do not react to short-term drops.**
Markets go down. Sometimes significantly. If your time horizon is 10+ years, a market decline is a temporary event, not a permanent loss — unless you sell. Most investors who check their accounts less frequently during downturns make better decisions than those who watch daily.

**Step 5: Increase contributions as income grows.**
When you get a raise, increase your contribution percentage before you adjust your spending. Lifestyle inflation — spending more as you earn more — is one of the biggest obstacles to long-term wealth building.
